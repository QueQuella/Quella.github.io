How Game Spaces Influence Players’ Immersion and Perceptions of Real-World Space

A Study of Exit 8-Style Games · Individual Research Proposal

Status: theoretical research proposal · Hypotheses have not been empirically tested.

01 / Abstract

When familiar places become unfamiliar

A subway corridor or a hotel hallway is usually a space we pass through without much attention. In games such as The Exit 8, Next Business Trip and Room 13, familiar transitional spaces become environments of repetition, observation and uncertainty. Players move through apparently ordinary settings, identify subtle anomalies and reconsider what they expect to see. This proposal asks how the overlap between virtual and everyday spaces may shape immersion and enjoyment, and whether that experience may influence how players perceive similar places outside the game.

The study brings together spatial presence and flow, Goffman’s frame analysis, and Foucault’s concept of heterotopia. These perspectives provide a way to consider how players feel located within a game space, become absorbed in its actions and reinterpret familiar settings through the frame of play. Rather than treating the virtual environment as a background, the proposal considers space as an active part of the experience: recognition establishes familiarity, while unexpected differences invite closer attention.

Three hypotheses connect spatial overlap with immersion and enjoyment, and then with possible changes in real-world spatial perception and attitudes toward everyday habits. The proposal develops a theoretical model for further investigation. Its central interest is whether an experience designed around noticing small changes can extend beyond the screen, encouraging players to look at ordinary places differently.

02 / Research Questions

What might gameplay carry into daily life?

  1. How does the overlap between game spaces and familiar real-world spaces relate to players’ immersion and enjoyment?
  2. How might immersion and enjoyment influence players’ perceptions of similar spaces in everyday life?
  3. How might these experiences relate to players’ attitudes toward changing their everyday habits?
03 / Theoretical Framework

Three ways to understand the experience

Spatial Presence & Flow

Presence concerns the feeling of being in the game environment; flow concerns absorption in the activity. The proposal uses both to consider players’ psychological engagement and enjoyment.

Frame Analysis

Goffman’s approach helps examine how rules and unexpected events may shift the way a familiar space is interpreted, from an ordinary setting into a space of play.

Heterotopia

Foucault’s concept offers a perspective on the coexistence of familiar and impossible spaces, and how a game environment may invite reflection on everyday places.

04 / Proposed Research Model

From spatial familiarity to possible change

Overlap between game and real-world spaces→Immersion & enjoyment→Spatial perceptions & attitudes toward habits

A proposed relationship for investigation.

H1 / Spatial overlap
The overlap between the game world and the real world is proposed to have a positive effect on players’ immersion and enjoyment.
H2 / Spatial perception
Greater immersion and enjoyment are proposed to be associated with a greater likelihood of changes in players’ perceptions of real-world spaces.
H3 / Everyday habits
Greater immersion and enjoyment are proposed to be associated with more positive attitudes toward changing everyday habits.
05 / Reflection

Connecting research with creative practice

Developing this proposal helped me connect game-space design with questions about attention, immersion and everyday experience. It gave me a way to examine the relationship between a designed environment and the meanings players bring to it, rather than discussing atmosphere only as a visual quality.

For my creative practice, the proposal raises a question: how can familiar spaces and small disruptions encourage people to observe more closely? The current work establishes research questions and hypotheses; testing them would require a defined research design, measures and participant data.
